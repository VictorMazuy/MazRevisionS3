2.1Game theory: the vocabulary

Game

A situation where the decision-makers take into account interdependence.

Players

The (rational) decision-makers.

Pure strategies

The possible actions of the players. In this course choices are discrete: only a limited number of output levels can be chosen.

Payoff matrix

The outcomes for each player for each possible combination of strategies. Convention: (player 1 ; player 2), player 1 in rows.

Assumptions about players
  • Individualistic: driven by self-interest, striving for the best outcome for themselves (profit, sales).
  • Rational, and each understands the rules of the game.
  • They play a one-shot simultaneous game: once, at the same moment.

Cartel agreement (course definition): firms agree to cooperate and coordinate on quantity and/or price to maximize the sum of their profits, as if they were one merged monopolist. In the duopoly game: maintain the cartel = low output (keeps the price high); break the cartel = high output.

The slides add two managerial perspectives: game theory = how to play the game (which strategy maximizes profit given the strategic interaction); mechanism design = how to design the game (which market conditions to install, e.g. auctions).

2.1.1The prisoner's dilemma

Two suspects, interrogated separately, cannot confer. Payoffs are years in prison, so lower is better.

Dominant strategy

A strategy that always produces the best result for that player, regardless of what the other player does. If a player has a dominant strategy, all their other strategies are dominated.

Prisoner 1: if prisoner 2 confesses, confess (8 < 10 years); if prisoner 2 denies, confess (1 < 2 years). Confess is dominant, and the same holds for prisoner 2. Rational prisoner 2 also expects prisoner 1 to confess and can "delete" the dominated row; the order in which dominated strategies are deleted does not matter.

Expected solution: (s1, t1) = (confess, confess), 8 years each, while (deny, deny) would give 2 years each.

Central insight of game theory

The blind pursuit of self-interest does not necessarily lead to the best outcome for the individual himself.

2.1.2Equilibrium in dominant strategies

Equilibrium in dominant strategies

Each player plays his/her dominant strategy; the expected outcome consists of two dominant strategies. Rational players always play a dominant strategy and anticipate that others play theirs.

Table 2: break is best for duopolist 1 whether 2 maintains (4500 > 4000) or breaks (3000 > 2900); symmetric for duopolist 2. (Break, Break) is an equilibrium in dominant strategies, although (Maintain, Maintain) gives both more and maximizes the sum of profits.

2.1.3Nash equilibria

Nash equilibrium

A combination of strategies where no player wishes to change his strategy given the expected strategy of the other players.

Method (slides)

For each strategy of the other player, identify the best response. If both players' best responses coincide in the same cell, that cell is a Nash equilibrium. In practice: underline player 1's best payoff in each column and player 2's best payoff in each row; a cell with both underlined is a Nash equilibrium.

Relationship

  • Every equilibrium in dominant strategies is a Nash equilibrium; not every Nash equilibrium is one in dominant strategies.
  • If there is an equilibrium in dominant strategies, it is also the unique Nash equilibrium.
  • The Nash equilibrium is the more general concept.

Expected solution

  • Nash equilibria need not be unique.
  • A unique Nash equilibrium is the expected solution.
  • If there is no unique Nash equilibrium, there is no expected solution in the absence of coordination.

2.2Cooperation, social norms and contracts (climate)

The prisoner's-dilemma insight applies to cartels, OPEC output limits, free-riding in group projects and CO₂ agreements (Kyoto, Paris).

Battle of the sexes: two Nash equilibria

No dominant strategy for either player. Two Nash equilibria: (Football, Football) and (Cinema, Cinema). It illustrates: (1) the importance of coordinated strategies, (2) simultaneous vs sequential choice, (3) a Nash equilibrium does not have to be unique.

CO₂ scenario 1 (Table 6)

Two NE: (reduce, reduce) and (not reduce, not reduce). Neither is the expected solution.

CO₂ scenario 2 (Table 7)

A prisoner's dilemma: unique NE (not reduce, not reduce).

Scenario 1: three avenues to reach the superior NE without enforceable agreements
  1. Make the game sequential: it is easier to coordinate, and there is a first-mover advantage (the EU moving first with the Green Deal can pull the US and Asia along).
  2. Cooperation: a bilateral (multilateral) agreement. Even non-enforceable, it leads to the desirable NE here, because it is an equilibrium.
  3. Social norms (e.g. "never break an agreement"), which impose the cooperative outcome from outside, like a binding agreement.
Scenario 2: "If you can't win the game, change the rules"

When the desired outcome is not an equilibrium, non-binding agreements end in false promises. The way out is mechanism design: change the structure and payoffs of the game. Make unilateral defection costly (CO₂ taxes, consumer action, social awareness), e.g. lower the defection payoff from 10 to 3. That turns Table 7 into Table 6, where (reduce, reduce) is a possible NE reachable by agreements or sequential choice. Police interrogate suspects separately and simultaneously on purpose: that design makes (confess, confess) the expected solution.

The course discusses non-cooperative game theory: binding agreements are assumed impossible. Cooperative game theory assumes agreements are implemented.

2.3The game of Big Tech: beyond one-shot simultaneous games

Maintain–break game and antitrust

Breaking is dominant for both; (Break, Break) is the unique NE although both prefer (Maintain, Maintain). EU competition law (Article 101 TFEU) prohibits cartels: price fixing, restricting output, dividing markets. But prohibiting explicit agreements does not necessarily produce the competitive outcome, because firms interact repeatedly.

Repeated games: firms are there to stay

Repeated game

The same players face the same or a closely related strategic situation more than once.

  • Breaking gives an immediate gain (9000 > 8000), but the rival may retaliate, leaving 2000 per period instead of 8000.
  • Known final period: in the last period both break (no future retaliation possible); reasoning backwards, cooperation unravels in earlier periods too.
  • No known final period (indefinitely repeated): current behaviour affects a long stream of future payoffs.
Tit-for-tat

Begin by maintaining; maintain if the rival maintained in the preceding period; break if the rival broke in the preceding period. It rewards cooperation and responds to deviation. When future profits matter enough, (Maintain, Maintain) is sustainable even though (Break, Break) is the unique NE of the one-shot game. This depends on the payoffs, the observability of behaviour, and how much firms value future profits.

Slides: tit-for-tat escapes an inferior NE only if the game is repeated many (indefinitely many) times. Antitrust law may be ineffective when Big Tech firms play indefinitely, because repeated interaction enables tacit collusion sustained by reputation and retaliation, without any communication.
